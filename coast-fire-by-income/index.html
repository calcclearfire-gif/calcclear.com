Coast FIRE by Income: What You Actually Need Saved by 30

Most Coast FIRE calculators ask for one number and hand you one answer. This one works through five income brackets with explicit assumptions, shows the math, and compares Coast FIRE against full FIRE so you can see what the trade-off really costs.

What Coast FIRE actually means

Full FIRE means saving 25× your annual expenses and never working again. Coast FIRE is the intermediate version: save enough now that compound growth alone carries you to your full retirement number by a conventional age — say 65 — so you can still work a job you don't hate, on a schedule you choose, without another contribution to the portfolio.

The definition sounds simple and hides three assumptions that change the answer more than anything else:

  • The runway. How many years of pure growth stand between your savings and the 25× target. Every extra year of runway roughly halves what you need.
  • The real return. We use 7% after inflation — a historical long-run stock-heavy portfolio estimate. At 4% the numbers below grow 20–25%. At 5% they grow roughly 40%. This assumption dominates everything else.
  • Spending in retirement. We assume your retirement spending equals today's spending, in today's dollars. If your costs rise as you age (healthcare, housing), the target rises with them.

The five brackets, worked out

For each income level we assume a plausible savings rate, take the remaining spending as the annual retirement budget, multiply by 25 (the 4% safe-withdrawal convention), and discount that target 35 years at 7% to get the amount a 30-year-old would need in the account today.

Three observations that surprise people

1. The coast number scales with spending, not income

A $250k earner who saves 40% and spends $150k a year needs $351k to coast — 3.3× the $105k needed by the $50k earner. Income alone tells you nothing. Two people with identical salaries can have coast numbers that differ by 2×, purely on lifestyle. If you're early in your career, the highest-leverage decision you can make is not which fund to buy — it's keeping the spending side of that ratio under control.

2. Coast FIRE is front-loaded; that's the point

Notice the last column requires saving at 20–40% of income for a concentrated five years. That's the trade: you compress the effort into a window where your career cash flow is building, then stop. The alternative — full FIRE at $100k income with a 20% savings rate — takes 31 years of never-stopping contributions to reach $2,000,000. Coast FIRE converts 31 years of grinding into 5 years of intensity and 30 years of optionality. Neither is objectively better; they're different risk profiles. The coast profile bets on 35 years of market survival; the full profile bets on 31 years of your own discipline.

3. The 4% rule is doing more work than it looks

Every number above inherits the 4% safe-withdrawal assumption, which comes from a specific historical US equity drawdown pattern. If you expect a worse sequence — the 1970s, for instance — practitioners often quote 3%–3.5%, which would raise every coast number in the table by roughly 40–65%. The table is the base case, not a ceiling.

What Coast FIRE does not cover

  • Sequence-of-returns risk. A bad decade at year 2 of 35 is survivable. A bad decade at year 33 is not, because there's no time to recover. The longer your coast, the more you're relying on the early years being okay.
  • Human-capital risk. The plan assumes you can work a low-stress job for 35 years. Industries that compress, burn out, or automate away roles invalidate the premise regardless of what the portfolio does.
  • Taxes and withdrawal mechanics. The 25× multiple is pre-tax. Your actual withdrawal tax bill (Roth vs pre-tax mix, state of residence, required minimum distributions) determines how much of that $1,125,000 is truly spendable.

How to use the numbers

Find your row. If the coast number is within reach of a 3–5 year save burst, Coast FIRE is a legitimate strategy — not a consolation prize. If it's 3× your current net worth and your income isn't on a steep climb, the honest answer is usually "full FIRE later" rather than "coast now and hope." Either way, the number you should be tracking is not your salary. It's the gap between your current savings and the number in your row, divided by your remaining runway.
